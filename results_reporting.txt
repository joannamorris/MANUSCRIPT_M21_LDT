1. Framing in the Methods

In the Participants or Individual Differences subsection, describe the PCA-based grouping carefully:

To assess individual differences in linguistic sensitivity, participants completed three standardized tasks indexing spelling, vocabulary, and print exposure (Author Recognition Test). Following Andrews and Lo (2013), scores were submitted to a principal components analysis (PCA). The first two components accounted for most of the variance (PCA1 = 52%, PCA2 = 31%). PCA1 loaded primarily on vocabulary and print exposure, while PCA2 loaded primarily on spelling ability. Participants were categorized according to whether their standardized PCA scores were above or below zero on each component, yielding two independent dimensions of individual differences: semantic sensitivity (PCA1) and orthographic sensitivity (PCA2).

Then make clear that these dimensions are orthogonal, not separate experiments:

Because the two components reflect partially independent sources of variance (semantic vs. orthographic sensitivity), we examined the effects of each dimension separately in parallel analyses. The same ERP data were used for both sets of analyses, but the grouping factor differed (High vs. Low Semantic Sensitivity; High vs. Low Orthographic Sensitivity).

That one sentence (“The same ERP data were used…”) is crucial for transparency.

⸻

2. Structuring in the Results

You have a few legitimate reporting options, depending on space and focus:

Option A: Separate sections, but explicitly parallel

For instance:
    - 3.1 Effects of Orthographic Sensitivity
    - 3.2 Effects of Semantic Sensitivity

And in the introductory sentence to 3.2:

The following analyses use the same dataset described above, but participants were divided according to their PCA1 (semantic sensitivity) scores rather than PCA2 (orthographic sensitivity). This complementary analysis tests whether individual differences in semantic knowledge modulate the same ERP effects that varied with orthographic sensitivity.

This signals that these are alternative lenses on the same data, not new experiments.

Option B: Combined reporting with subheadings

If space is tight or you want to emphasize convergence/divergence:

We next examined whether the pattern of ERP effects varied as a function of individual differences in either orthographic or semantic sensitivity (derived from PCA1 and PCA2). To do so, we repeated the mixed-effects analyses using each PCA-derived grouping factor separately.

You could then include a table summarizing significant effects for each grouping.

3. Addressing possible concerns about “double dipping”

It’s perfectly acceptable to perform separate models with different theoretically motivated grouping variables as long as you:
    - Clearly explain that the same underlying ERP dataset was used;
    - Acknowledge that these analyses are exploratory or complementary rather than independent replications;
    - Avoid interpreting both sets of analyses as additive evidence for the same claim.

If you want to be extra rigorous, you can explicitly write in the Statistical Analysis section:

Because the two PCA dimensions were statistically independent, separate mixed-effects models were fit to the same ERP data with either Orthographic Sensitivity or Semantic Sensitivity as the between-subjects factor. These analyses should therefore be interpreted as complementary rather than cumulative.

This makes it clear that you are not “double counting” the same data.

Statistical Analysis 

ERP amplitudes were analyzed using linear mixed-effects models (LMMs) implemented in the lme4 package (Bates et al., 2015) in R. Separate models were fit for the N250 and N400 time windows and for word and non-word stimuli. Each model predicted mean amplitude as a function of Sensitivity (either Semantic_Sensitivity or Orthographic_Sensitivity), family_size, and complexity, including all interactions among these fixed effects. Random effects included by-subject intercepts and slopes for family_size and complexity, and random intercepts for electrode site (chlabel) nested within subject. The model formula was:

value ~ Sensitivity * family_size * complexity +
         (1 + family_size + complexity ¦ SubjID) +
         (1 ¦ SubjID:chlabel)

Degrees of freedom and p-values were estimated using the Kenward–Roger approximation (afex; Singmann et al., 2023). When higher-order interactions were significant, they were explored using estimated marginal means (EMMs) and contrasts computed with emmeans (Lenth, 2024). For each interaction, we first obtained the full EMM grid (e.g., emmeans(model, ~ family_size * base_freq)), followed by targeted simple-effects comparisons using pairwise contrasts within each level of the interacting factor. This approach allowed us to estimate the effect of family_size at each level of base_freq, and vice versa, as well as to compute interaction contrasts (difference-of-differences) that quantify how one factor’s effect changes across the levels of another (e.g., contrast(emm, interaction = "pairwise")).

For each simple or interaction contrast, we reported the estimated mean difference, standard error, t-ratio, p-value, and 95 % confidence interval. Effect sizes (Cohen’s d based on the residual sigma of the fitted model) were computed for all pairwise comparisons using the eff_size() function in emmeans. In three-way interactions, follow-ups were conducted hierarchically: first examining two-way simple effects (e.g., family_size × base_freq within each Sensitivity group), then comparing those two-way effects across levels of the third factor to obtain difference-of-differences-of-differences. Multiple-comparison adjustments were applied (Holm correction) when testing sets of related contrasts, but exploratory plots and unadjusted follow-ups were also inspected to aid interpretation.

All analyses therefore provide both directional and quantitative evidence for the patterns underlying significant interactions, reported as estimated marginal means with their 95 % confidence intervals and associated standardized effect sizes.

Results Overview

ERP amplitudes were analyzed separately for the N250 (150–250 ms) and N400 (300–500 ms) time windows, and for word and non-word stimuli. Each analysis used the same mixed-effects model structure described above, differing only in the between-subjects grouping variable—Orthographic_Sensitivity or Semantic_Sensitivity—derived from the PCA of individual-difference measures. These parallel analyses therefore represent complementary tests applied to the same ERP data, probing whether morphological processing varies with individual differences in spelling-based versus meaning-based linguistic knowledge.

For each time window, separate models were fit to the word and non-word datasets, which differed in their within-subject predictors. The word models included family_size and base_frequency, along with their interactions with Sensitivity. The non-word models included family_size and morphological_complexity and their corresponding interactions with Sensitivity. All models contained by-subject random intercepts and slopes for the relevant within-subject factors, as well as random intercepts for electrode site (chlabel) nested within subject. Model significance tests used the Kenward–Roger approximation for degrees of freedom, and significant effects were followed up using estimated marginal means (EMMs) and pairwise contrasts.

The following sections report the effects of Orthographic Sensitivity first, followed by the corresponding analyses for Semantic Sensitivity. Results are interpreted as distinct but convergent perspectives on the same underlying dataset, highlighting how differences in orthographic and semantic proficiency shape morphological processing during visual word recognition.

When interactions were significant, they were unpacked using a hierarchical follow-up procedure tailored to the relevant within-subject factors. In the word analyses, the fixed factors were Sensitivity (Semantic or Orthographic), family_size, and base_frequency; in the non-word analyses, they were Sensitivity, family_size, and morphological_complexity. Two-way interactions were examined by estimating the simple effect of one factor within each level of the other (e.g., the base-frequency effect separately for large- and small-family words) and then computing a difference-of-differences contrast to test whether those simple effects differed. For three-way interactions, this procedure was extended hierarchically: the relevant two-way interaction was first examined within each level of Sensitivity and then compared across High and Low groups. This approach provides a clear statistical and conceptual framework for describing how morphological variables interact with individual differences in orthographic or semantic sensitivity.
