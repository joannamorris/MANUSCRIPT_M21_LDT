Research on morphological processing has long relied on morphologically complex nonwords to investigate how readers represent and process internal word structure. Nonwords provide a particularly valuable methodological tool because they allow researchers to examine morphological processing independently of stored lexical representations. If readers exhibit sensitivity to the internal structure of a novel form such as `farmify` or `teachment`, such effects cannot be explained solely by retrieval of a whole-word lexical entry. Instead, they suggest that the language-processing system is capable of generalizing beyond previously encountered words and computing structure online.

A substantial body of research demonstrates that morphologically structured nonwords are processed differently from orthographically similar but morphologically implausible strings. Morphologically plausible nonwords often produce faster lexical decision responses, morphological priming effects, ERP signatures associated with decomposition, stem-frequency effects, and sensitivity to affix productivity. Such findings have traditionally been interpreted as evidence that readers automatically segment complex forms into constituent morphemes during visual word recognition. On this view, morphology constitutes an independent representational level within the lexical processing system, and readers decompose complex forms into stems and affixes even when the resulting form is novel or semantically anomalous.

However, the interpretation of these findings remains a matter of theoretical debate. Classical decompositional approaches argue that the processing advantages observed for morphologically structured nonwords reflect rule-governed parsing mechanisms that operate over explicitly represented morphemes. According to these accounts, readers possess abstract knowledge of morphological structure and use this knowledge productively during word recognition. The ability to interpret novel forms such as `rewalkable` or `microteachability` is therefore taken as evidence that morphological processing is generative and combinatorial rather than reducible to whole-word memorization.

In contrast, connectionist and discriminative learning approaches have argued that many apparent morphological effects may emerge from learned statistical regularities in the mapping between orthographic, phonological, and semantic patterns, without requiring explicit morphemic representations. From this perspective, a novel form such as `farmify` is processed efficiently because it resembles previously encountered words ending in `-ify` and therefore activates a learned distributional pattern associated with verbal derivations. Similarly, effects attributed to decomposition may instead reflect graded similarity relationships among orthographic and semantic representations. On such accounts, morphology is not necessarily a primitive representational unit, but rather an emergent property of the statistical structure of language.

Importantly, morphologically complex nonwords remain informative regardless of which theoretical framework is adopted. For decompositional theories, they provide a means of demonstrating productive rule-based parsing in the absence of stored lexical representations. For connectionist and discriminative approaches, they provide a critical test of how statistical regularities generalize to novel inputs. In both cases, nonwords make it possible to distinguish processing driven by prior lexical experience from processing driven by broader generalizations over linguistic structure. As a result, the study of morphologically complex nonwords has become central to contemporary debates concerning the nature of morphological representation, the extent to which decomposition is obligatory or graded, and the relationship between morphology and statistical learning in visual word recognition.
