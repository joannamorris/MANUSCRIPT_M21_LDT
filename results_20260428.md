### What This Pattern Means Theoretically

This is your most important theoretical finding at the N250 and it has two components that need to be addressed directly in the write-up.

**First, the reversal at high skill.** At +1 SD of both OS and LP, simple nonwords become *more negative* than complex nonwords. This means highly skilled readers are showing the opposite of what a processing cost account would predict — complex nonwords are *easier* to process early than simple ones. The most plausible interpretation is that for skilled readers, a real suffix provides reliable form-level information that guides early lexical access even in the absence of a stored whole-word entry. The real suffix in *gasful* points the system efficiently toward a morphological parse; the pseudosuffix in *gasfil* provides no such signal, leaving the system without a clear parsing route and producing greater early uncertainty. This is consistent with discriminative learning — high OS and LP readers have more precisely calibrated n-gram weights, so the real suffix cues produce stronger and more reliable activation than the pseudosuffix cues.

**Second, both OS and LP modulate the N250.** Your introduction predicted that OS would be the primary driver of early effects. The data show that LP is equally involved. Rather than treating this as a failure of the prediction, the parallel pattern across the two dimensions suggests they are both indexing aspects of accumulated lexical experience that contribute to early morphological parsing efficiency. The Discussion should address why LP — which you framed as primarily a semantic variable — is modulating an early form-based component.
