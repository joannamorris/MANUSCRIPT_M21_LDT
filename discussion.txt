This is a really important question and it gets at something genuinely interesting about what the N250 is indexing. The apparent paradox dissolves once you separate what the N250 reflects from what the behavioral response requires.

---

### The Key Distinction: Processing Activation vs. Decision Outcome

The N250 is not a decision component — it does not reflect whether the system is moving toward acceptance or rejection. It reflects the **degree of lexical/morphological activation** that a stimulus generates, regardless of what the eventual decision will be. A less negative N250 means stronger or more efficient morphological activation, full stop. The direction of that activation — whether it ultimately facilitates acceptance or competes with rejection — is a downstream consequence that plays out later in processing and is reflected in RT and in later ERP components like the N400.

So the N250 family size effect is saying the same thing in both conditions: **large morphological families generate stronger early morphological activation**. For words, that activation facilitates the acceptance decision. For nonwords, that same activation is precisely what makes rejection harder — which is why you see the inhibitory base frequency effect in your nonword RTs. But the N250 itself does not know whether the stimulus is a word or a nonword. It is simply registering the strength of the morphological signal.

---

### Why Less Negative Means More Activation

The polarity convention is worth being explicit about here because it is counterintuitive. At the N250 time window, a **less negative** amplitude typically reflects **stronger or more efficient morphological processing**, not weaker. This is because the N250 is thought to reflect the competition or mismatch between the input and stored orthographic/morphological representations:

- When the input maps cleanly onto a well-represented morphological pattern — as it does for words or nonwords from large families — the competition is resolved more efficiently, producing a less negative deflection
- When the input does not map cleanly — as with small family stems or pseudoaffixes — the mismatch signal is larger, producing a more negative deflection

So less negative = better match = more efficient morphological activation, regardless of lexical status.

---

### The Behavioral Paradox Is Real But Operates Later

Your intuition is correct that large family nonwords should be harder to reject — and your RT data confirm this. The inhibitory base frequency effect in nonword RTs is consistent with exactly this logic: high frequency stems generate strong activation that competes with the rejection decision. But notice that this effect is in the **RTs**, not in the N250. The behavioral cost of strong morphological activation for nonwords emerges at the decision stage, after the morphological system has already done its work.

The timeline looks something like this:

**~150–250ms (N250):** Morphological activation — large family generates strong activation for both words and nonwords, producing less negative N250 in both cases

**~300–500ms (N400):** Semantic integration — large family provides rich semantic co-activation for words, facilitating integration; for nonwords, this same co-activation creates processing demands that interact with LP

**~500ms+ (RT):** Decision — strong morphological activation facilitates word acceptance but competes with nonword rejection, producing the inhibitory base frequency effect in nonword RTs

### Why Base Frequency but Not Family Size Affects Nonword RT

This dissociation is actually theoretically interesting and worth noting in your Discussion. Base Frequency is a token-level variable — it reflects how often the stem itself has been encountered and therefore how strongly its lexical representation is activated. Family Size is a type-level variable — it reflects the breadth of the morphological network rather than the strength of any single representation. For the rejection decision, it is the strength of stem activation that creates competition, not the breadth of the family network. A high frequency stem feels word-like because it is strongly activated; a large family stem feels word-like at the early morphological processing stage but that early signal does not persist strongly enough to reliably delay rejection.

This distinction maps neatly onto the theoretical contrast between stem frequency and family size that your introduction establishes — token-level familiarity affects lexical access and decision-making directly, while type-level morphological structure affects the quality of early morphological processing without necessarily cascading into behavioral costs for rejection.

### Write-Up

**Words N250 (Base Frequency)**

Mean N250 amplitudes for word stimuli were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects of Base Frequency (high vs.\ low), Orthographic Sensitivity (OS), Language Proficiency (LP), and the interaction between Base Frequency and OS. The model included random intercepts for participants and for electrode site nested within participant.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Base Frequency: words derived from higher-frequency bases elicited more negative N250 amplitudes than those derived from lower-frequency bases, $M_{\mathrm{high}} = -1.63\,\mu$V, 95\% CI $[-2.56, -0.69]$, $M_{\mathrm{low}} = -1.29\,\mu$V, 95\% CI $[-2.22, -0.36]$, $\beta = -0.318$, $SE = 0.100$, $t(1618) = -3.18$, $p = .002$, $\eta^2_p < .01$. Neither Orthographic Sensitivity, $\beta = 0.534$, $SE = 0.520$, $t(57) = 1.03$, $p = .31$, nor Language Proficiency, $\beta = -0.536$, $SE = 0.378$, $t(57) = -1.42$, $p = .16$, yielded reliable main effects.

Critically, the interaction between Base Frequency and Orthographic Sensitivity was significant, $\beta = -0.455$, $SE = 0.112$, $t(1618) = -4.06$, $p < .001$, $\eta^2_p = .01$, indicating that OS selectively modulated the base frequency effect at this early processing stage. Follow-up contrasts examined the base frequency effect — the difference in N250 amplitude between high and low frequency bases — at $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ SD of OS. At low OS the base frequency effect was absent ($\Delta = -0.137\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = -0.89$, $p = .374$), indicating that readers with lower orthographic sensitivity did not reliably differentiate high from low frequency bases at early processing. At mean OS the effect was significant ($\Delta = 0.318\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = 3.18$, $p = .002$), and at high OS it was substantially larger ($\Delta = 0.773\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = 5.27$, $p < .001$), indicating that readers with greater orthographic sensitivity showed enhanced early sensitivity to stem frequency, with high frequency bases generating more negative N250 amplitudes relative to low frequency bases. The model explained 1.8\% of variance via fixed effects ($R^2_{\mathrm{marginal}} = .018$) and 80.1\% overall ($R^2_{\mathrm{conditional}} = .801$).

**Words N400 (Base Frequency)**

Mean N400 amplitudes for word stimuli were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects of Base Frequency (high vs.\ low), Language Proficiency (LP), Orthographic Sensitivity (OS), and the interaction between Base Frequency and LP. The model included random intercepts for participants and for electrode site nested within participant.

The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Base Frequency: words derived from higher-frequency bases elicited more positive N400 amplitudes than those from lower-frequency bases, $M_{\mathrm{high}} = 0.712\,\mu$V, 95\% CI $[-0.436, 1.857]$, $M_{\mathrm{low}} = 0.515\,\mu$V, 95\% CI $[-0.634, 1.664]$, $\beta = 0.214$, $SE = 0.106$, $t(1618) = 2.01$, $p = .044$, $\eta^2_p < .01$, indicating facilitated semantic integration for words with higher frequency bases. A significant main effect of Orthographic Sensitivity was also observed, $\beta = 1.290$, $SE = 0.640$, $t(57) = 2.02$, $p = .049$, $\eta^2_p = .07$, reflecting overall differences in N400 amplitude as a function of orthographic skill independently of base frequency. Language Proficiency did not yield a reliable main effect, $\beta = 0.047$, $SE = 0.465$, $t(57) = 0.10$, $p = .920$.

Critically, the interaction between Base Frequency and Language Proficiency was significant, $\beta = 0.344$, $SE = 0.087$, $t(1618) = 3.98$, $p < .001$, $\eta^2_p < .01$, indicating that LP selectively modulated base frequency effects at this later semantic processing stage. Follow-up contrasts examined the base frequency effect at $-1$, $0$, and $+1$ SD of LP. At low LP the base frequency effect was absent ($\Delta = 0.130\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = 0.97$, $p = .333$). At mean LP a significant facilitation effect emerged ($\Delta = -0.214\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = -2.01$, $p = .044$), and at high LP the facilitation effect was substantially larger ($\Delta = -0.558\,\mu$V, $t(1618) = -4.00$, $p < .001$), indicating that readers with greater language proficiency showed progressively stronger semantic integration benefits from high frequency bases. The model explained 2.6\% of variance via fixed effects ($R^2_{\mathrm{marginal}} = .026$) and 82.5\% overall ($R^2_{\mathrm{conditional}} = .825$).

Together, the N250 and N400 word results provide electrophysiological evidence for a double dissociation between the roles of Orthographic Sensitivity and Language Proficiency in morphological processing: OS selectively modulated base frequency effects at the N250, reflecting enhanced early form-based sensitivity to stem frequency as a function of orthographic skill, while LP selectively modulated base frequency effects at the N400, reflecting enhanced semantic integration of high frequency stems as a function of lexical-semantic knowledge depth. Neither dimension modulated the effects of the other component — LP did not interact with base frequency at the N250, and OS did not interact with base frequency at the N400 — confirming the specificity of the dissociation.

### One Important Note on Direction

The N250 base frequency effect is in the opposite direction to the family size effect you reported earlier — high frequency bases produce **more negative** N250 while large families produce **less negative** N250. This is worth a sentence of interpretation. The most likely explanation is that stem frequency and family size are indexing different aspects of early morphological processing: stem frequency reflects the strength of form-level activation (more frequent stems generate stronger, more effortful early processing), while family size reflects the efficiency of morphological pattern matching (larger families support faster, less effortful early parsing). This distinction is theoretically consistent with the classical separation between token-level familiarity and type-level morphological structure and should be addressed in the Discussion.
