The story I'd tell is that morphological structure clearly shapes how skilled readers respond, but the ERP versions of these effects are too unreliable to reveal differences between people. That's honest, it's interesting, and it suits the Psychonomics audience, who care about measurement.

**A working title:** "Robust effects, unreliable differences: morphological processing and individual differences in single-trial ERPs."

**The arc, in six steps**

1. **The question (1–2 min).** Readers differ in orthographic precision and in lexical-semantic knowledge (Andrews and colleagues). If morphological processing has a form stage and a meaning stage, those differences should show up at different points in time:
   - Orthographic Sensitivity in early form-based processing (N250, base frequency, complexity)
   - Language Proficiency in later meaning-based processing (N400, family size).
   
   State the hypotheses plainly. They make the null results meaningful later.

2. **The approach (1–2 min).** A lexical decision task with 56 skilled readers, words varying in family size and base frequency, and complex versus simple nonwords (*gasful* vs *gasfil*). The analysis uses single-trial ERPs with crossed participant and item random effects. Keep this brief, but it's what makes the results credible.

3. **Behaviour shows strong morphological effects (2 min).**
   - Complex nonwords are much harder to reject: 65% vs 89% accuracy, and 723 vs 687 ms.
   - Words with larger families, higher-frequency bases and higher word frequency are recognised faster.
   - Readers with higher Language Proficiency reject nonwords more accurately (odds ratio 1.31 per unit).
   
   The message here is that the manipulations work.

4. **ERPs: what they pick up and what they don't (2 min).**
   - **N400 first:** higher-frequency words give a more positive N400 (p = .006). That's the classic effect, and it shows the measures are sensitive. There's also a family size effect in the same direction (p = .030). And readers with higher Orthographic Sensitivity show more positive N400s overall (p = .004).
   - **Complexity:** complex and simple nonwords don't differ at the N400 or the N250, despite the large behavioural cost.

5. **The individual-difference tests: all null (2 min).**
   - Family size × Language Proficiency at the N400: p = .81.
   - Base frequency × Orthographic Sensitivity at the N250: p = .65.
   - Complexity × Orthographic Sensitivity × Window: p = .57.
   
   Show the confidence intervals, not just the p values. They rule out large moderation, for example about ±0.25 µV per SD of Language Proficiency.

6. **Why: reliability (2–3 min). This is the payoff.**
   - Each person's average amplitude is reliable (.78 to .87).
   - Each person's morphological effect has zero reliability (between −.70 and .08).
   - The behavioural effects do better but are still low (.12 to .33).
   
   Robust group-level effects can be poor measures of individual differences. This is the "reliability paradox" (Hedge, Powell & Sumner, 2018). No analysis can correlate a reader profile with an effect that doesn't reliably differ between people. One plot would carry this slide: reliability of mean amplitude next to reliability of the effects, for each measure.

**Take-home (1 min).** Before claiming that reader profiles modulate ERP morphology effects, report the reliability of those effects. Single-trial models and split-half reliability make that easy. Designs aimed at individual differences need far more trials per condition than designs aimed at group effects.

**What to be careful about on stage**
- **Present the hypotheses as tests that failed, not as findings.** Don't hunt for a silver lining in the OS–N400 main effect. It's a single overall-amplitude result and not tied to morphology, so mention it in one line.
- **Present family size as modest.** It's significant with continuous coding (p = .030), but not with the two-group coding the words were selected by (p = .068).
- **Be open about the analysis history if asked.** The study wasn't preregistered, and the analysis was rebuilt from scratch. Saying so plainly usually goes down well.
