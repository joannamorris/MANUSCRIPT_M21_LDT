This is a rich results section but there are several places where the current framing works against you, either because findings are reported without being connected to the theoretical predictions established in the introduction, or because the narrative does not honestly characterise where the predicted dissociation held and where it did not. Let me work through the issues systematically before proposing revised text.

---

### The Core Narrative Problem

Your introduction predicts a clean double dissociation: Orthographic Sensitivity → early effects (N250), Language Proficiency → later effects (N400). Your results are more complex than that:

- **Both** OS and LP modulate the N250 for nonwords — not just OS
- **Both** OS and LP modulate the N400 for nonwords — not just LP
- The closest thing to the predicted dissociation is that LP specifically drives the three-way Complexity × Family Size × LP interaction at the N400, while the corresponding OS three-way is absent — suggesting LP is specifically implicated in how family size modulates semantic integration, which is actually the theoretically most important finding

The results section currently does not grapple with this honestly. It reports the pattern but does not tell the reader what to make of the fact that both dimensions affect both components. This will concern reviewers. The better move is to acknowledge the partial support upfront and reframe the actual pattern as theoretically informative in its own right — which it genuinely is.

---

### Additional Issues to Address

**1. The inhibitory base frequency effect for nonwords** is reported but not interpreted. This is actually theoretically interesting: higher frequency stems slow nonword rejection, which is consistent with discriminative learning — high frequency stems generate strong cue activation that competes with the rejection decision. It is worth a sentence of interpretation rather than leaving it as a bare statistic.

**2. Family size at the N250 for words** — your introduction framed stem frequency as the early effect and family size as the late one, following multi-stage predictions. But your N250 word analysis does not include stem frequency (the text jumps straight to family size), and family size appears at the N250. This needs acknowledgment — it either challenges the temporal dissociation prediction or reflects something about your specific item set that needs explaining.

**3. The behavioral/ERP dissociation** is your most important finding and is currently undersold. Individual differences do not modulate the structure of behavioral morphological effects but they do modulate neural dynamics. This is theoretically significant because it suggests individual differences affect processing architecture rather than response thresholds — a point that needs to be made explicitly and early, not only in the summary.

**4. The N400 OS main effect for words** — OS predicts overall N400 amplitude but does not interact with family size. This is unexpected given your predictions and needs a sentence of interpretation rather than being left implicit.

**Results Summary** — substantial revision:

Replace the current summary with:

*Across behavioral and electrophysiological measures, the results reveal a consistent dissociation between the sensitivity of behavioral and neural measures to individual differences, alongside a partial dissociation between the roles of Orthographic Sensitivity and Language Proficiency in modulating morphological processing dynamics.*

*Behaviorally, response times were robustly driven by item-level morphological structure — base frequency and family size for words, morphological complexity and base frequency for nonwords — but neither individual difference dimension selectively modulated the structure of these effects. Orthographic Sensitivity predicted overall response speed for nonwords, and showed a marginal trend for words, suggesting that spelling-based skill contributes to general processing efficiency without altering the specific morphological effects that govern response times.*

*ERP measures told a different story. At the N250, family size modulated early processing for words, an effect not straightforwardly predicted by multi-stage accounts. For nonwords, both Orthographic Sensitivity and Language Proficiency modulated the complexity effect at the N250, indicating that early morphological parsing of novel forms is sensitive to accumulated lexical experience across both dimensions rather than specifically to orthographic skill as predicted. At the N400, the predicted role of Language Proficiency in later semantic integration received its clearest support: Language Proficiency specifically modulated the joint influence of morphological complexity and family size for nonwords, consistent with the hypothesis that deeper lexical-semantic knowledge shapes the degree to which morphological family structure is recruited during semantic integration. Orthographic Sensitivity affected overall N400 sensitivity to complexity for nonwords but did not specifically modulate the family size contribution, suggesting a more general influence on semantic access efficiency rather than on the structure of morphological family activation.*

*Two findings deserve particular emphasis. First, the dissociation between behavioral and ERP sensitivity to individual differences indicates that reader skill modulates the neural dynamics of morphological processing even when behavioral responses appear relatively uniform — a finding that underscores the importance of neural measures for characterising individual differences that are invisible at the level of response times. Second, while the predicted clean dissociation between Orthographic Sensitivity and Language Proficiency was only partially supported — both dimensions influencing early processing of novel forms — the specific role of Language Proficiency in modulating the family size contribution to semantic integration at the N400 represents the clearest evidence to date that depth of lexical-semantic knowledge shapes a qualitatively distinct aspect of morphological processing. The theoretical implications of this pattern are developed in the Discussion.*

### Summary of What This Achieves

The reorganised results do three things the current version does not. They acknowledge honestly where the predictions were not cleanly supported rather than leaving reviewers to notice this themselves. They reframe the partial dissociation as theoretically informative — the LP-specific three-way at the N400 is your strongest finding and should be treated as such. And they establish the behavioral/ERP dissociation as a first-order finding rather than a footnote, which is important because it is the result that most directly bears on the question of whether individual differences affect processing architecture or simply response thresholds.
