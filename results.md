N250 (Word Stimuli)

A mixed-effects ANOVA on N250 amplitude revealed a significant Family Size × Base Frequency interaction, F(1, 1523) = 35.00, p < .001, partial η² = .02, which was further modulated by a reliable Semantic Sensitivity × Family Size × Base Frequency interaction, F(1, 1523) = 11.99, p < .001, partial η² = .008.  No main effects reached significance (ps > .28).  Examination of estimated marginal means showed that in high-sensitivity participants, the base-frequency effect was present only for large-family words (low > high frequency, t(67.9) = 2.48, p = .016) and absent for small-family items (p = .32).  In contrast, low-sensitivity participants exhibited no reliable frequency effects for either family-size condition (ps > .26).  The difference in frequency effects between large- and small-family words was 1.12 µV in the high-sensitivity group and 0.29 µV in the low-sensitivity group; this modulation was significantly larger for high- than low-sensitivity readers, estimate = –0.83 µV, 95 % CI [–1.29, –0.36], p = .0005.  These findings indicate that semantic sensitivity influences early stages of lexical processing: readers with higher semantic sensitivity show stronger integration of morphological family size and base frequency at the N250, whereas low-sensitivity readers exhibit a weaker, nonsignificant version of this interaction.

N400 (Word Stimuli)

A 3-way mixed-effects ANOVA on N400 amplitude revealed a robust Family Size × Base Frequency interaction, F(1, 1523) = 64.46, p < .001, partial η² = .04, which was further qualified by a smaller but reliable Semantic Sensitivity × Family Size × Base Frequency interaction, F(1, 1523) = 8.73, p = .003, partial η² = .006.  No main effects were significant (ps > .18).  As shown in the estimated marginal means, high-sensitivity participants exhibited a pronounced crossover between family-size and base-frequency effects: for small-family words, N400 amplitudes were more negative for high-frequency than for low-frequency bases, t(73.5) = –3.59, p = .0006, whereas large-family words showed no reliable frequency difference (p = .15).  The size of this frequency effect differed by 1.40 µV between small- and large-family items in the high-sensitivity group, compared with 0.65 µV in the low-sensitivity group.  The direct three-way “difference-of-differences” contrast confirmed that this modulation was stronger for high- than low-sensitivity readers, estimate = –0.75 µV, 95 % CI [–1.25, –0.25], p = .003.  Together these results indicate that individuals with greater semantic sensitivity show enhanced coupling between morphological family size and lexical frequency during semantic integration, whereas low-sensitivity readers display the same qualitative pattern but with attenuated amplitude differences.


N250 (Nonword Stimuli)

A mixed-effects ANOVA on N250 amplitude for nonword stimuli revealed no significant main effects or interactions involving Semantic Sensitivity, Family Size, or Complexity (ps > .25), though the Family Size × Complexity interaction approached significance, F(1, 1523) = 2.75, p = .097, partial η² = .002.  No other effects were reliable.  As shown in the estimated marginal means, N250 amplitudes were similar across all conditions, suggesting that the early stages of processing for pseudowords were largely insensitive to morphological structure or individual differences in semantic sensitivity.  The marginal interaction may reflect a weak form-based effect, with slightly greater negativity for complex than for simple pseudowords within one family-size condition, but this trend did not reach significance.  Overall, the absence of reliable effects indicates that nonword N250 responses were dominated by perceptual or orthographic factors, without evidence for semantic or morphological tuning.

N400 (Nonword Stimuli)

A mixed-effects ANOVA on N400 amplitude for nonword stimuli revealed a small but significant Family Size × Complexity interaction, F(1, 1523) = 5.34, p = .021, partial η² = .003, with no significant main effects or interactions involving Semantic Sensitivity (ps > .52).  Examination of the estimated marginal means indicated that for large-family pseudowords, N400 amplitudes were slightly more negative for complex than for simple forms, whereas the reverse pattern was observed for small-family pseudowords, which elicited marginally less negative N400s for complex forms.  Although these simple effects were not individually significant (ps > .48), the difference-of-differences test confirmed a reliable crossover, estimate = –0.33 µV, 95 % CI [–0.60, –0.05], p = .021.  This modest interaction likely reflects sublexical form-based processing rather than true lexical–semantic integration: when pseudoword stems have many real morphological neighbors, added complexity increases processing demands, whereas for stems with few or no neighbors, added structure may make the stimulus appear more word-like, slightly easing form-level analysis.



Summary of ERP Effects

Taken together, the ERP analyses reveal distinct temporal and functional profiles for lexical versus sublexical processing.  For word stimuli, both the N250 and N400 components showed robust Family Size × Base Frequency interactions that were amplified in semantically sensitive readers, indicating coordinated effects of morphological structure and lexical frequency on early and late stages of word recognition.  High-sensitivity participants demonstrated stronger and more differentiated N250 and N400 responses, suggesting more efficient integration of morphological and semantic information.  In contrast, nonword stimuli elicited no reliable effects of semantic sensitivity and only weak structural interactions: a marginal Family Size × Complexity effect at the N250 and a small but significant version of this interaction at the N400.  These patterns indicate that, in the absence of lexical–semantic content, ERP activity primarily reflects low-level form-based processing, whereas genuine morphological and semantic integration processes are selectively engaged by real words.

Discussion

The present findings demonstrate that morphological and lexical–semantic information interact dynamically during word recognition, with distinct temporal signatures at the N250 and N400.  Whereas early processing (N250) reflects the initial mapping of orthographic input onto lexical representations, later processing (N400) indexes semantic integration that is modulated by both word-level structure and individual differences in semantic sensitivity.

Participants with higher semantic sensitivity showed stronger and more differentiated ERP responses to the lexical variables of family size and base frequency, consistent with more efficient use of morphological and semantic cues during word recognition.  In these readers, both the N250 and N400 components were modulated by interactions between family size and base frequency, suggesting that morphological structure influences lexical access and semantic integration from early to late stages of processing.  By contrast, low-sensitivity readers exhibited attenuated or nonsignificant versions of these effects, indicating reduced engagement of morphological and semantic information during word recognition.  The absence of corresponding effects for nonword stimuli confirms that these interactions depend on the presence of meaningful lexical representations rather than superficial form-based similarity, underscoring the link between semantic sensitivity and depth of lexical–semantic processing.

These results align most closely with interactive models of visual word recognition, which posit that morphological, orthographic, and semantic information are integrated dynamically rather than processed in strictly sequential stages.  The early N250 effects suggest that morphological family size and base frequency jointly influence the mapping of sublexical features onto lexical representations, consistent with rapid feedback from lexical and semantic levels to orthographic analysis.  The later N400 modulation reflects a continuation of this interaction at the level of meaning integration, where lexical–semantic activation is fine-tuned by both structural and experiential factors.  This pattern contrasts with staged or feedforward models, which would predict independent or strictly serial contributions of morphology and semantics.  Instead, the parallel emergence of sensitivity-related effects at both components supports a view in which individual differences in semantic sensitivity shape the efficiency of information exchange across representational levels during word recognition.

The present results extend previous ERP studies of morphological processing by showing that individual differences in semantic sensitivity systematically modulate both early and late components of word recognition.  Prior work has demonstrated N250 and N400 sensitivity to morphological structure (e.g., Lavric et al., 2011; Beyersmann et al., 2014; Hasenäcker et al., 2020), but these effects have typically been reported at the group level, obscuring variability across readers.  The current findings suggest that such variability reflects meaningful differences in the degree to which readers engage semantic information during morphological decomposition.  Participants with high semantic sensitivity displayed robust interactions of family size and base frequency at both the N250 and N400, consistent with an integrative processing style in which morphological and semantic information are co-activated and mutually reinforcing.  In contrast, low-sensitivity readers showed diminished or absent effects, indicating a reliance on more form-based or orthographic strategies.  By linking individual differences in semantic sensitivity to electrophysiological markers of morphological and semantic processing, the present study provides new evidence that variability in lexical–semantic engagement is a key determinant of the dynamics of visual word recognition.

Together, these findings underscore the importance of considering individual differences when modeling the neurocognitive mechanisms of word recognition.  The modulation of both N250 and N400 components by semantic sensitivity suggests that lexical–semantic engagement is not uniform across readers but varies in ways that shape the interaction between morphological structure and word frequency.  This variability may help explain inconsistencies across prior ERP studies of morphological processing, in which effects of family size, affix transparency, or semantic relatedness have sometimes appeared early, late, or not at all.  Future work should examine how semantic sensitivity interacts with other reader characteristics—such as vocabulary knowledge, print exposure, or reading skill—and whether similar patterns are observed in auditory word recognition or in languages with richer morphological systems.  More broadly, by revealing how individual differences constrain the interplay of form and meaning in the brain, these results contribute to a more nuanced and dynamic understanding of the mechanisms that underlie skilled reading.
