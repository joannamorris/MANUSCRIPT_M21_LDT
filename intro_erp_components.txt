## The N250 as an Index of Sub-lexical Processing

A growing body of ERP research supports the interpretation that the N250 component reflects sub-lexical processing during visual word recognition. The N250 is a negative-going deflection peaking at approximately 250 ms post-stimulus onset, and it has been systematically dissociated from both earlier featural components and the later N400, which is broadly associated with lexical-semantic access (Holcomb & Grainger, 2006; Kutas & Federmeier, 2011).

The foundational evidence for a sub-lexical interpretation of the N250 comes from masked repetition priming studies. Holcomb and Grainger (2006) were the first to formally propose that the N250 reflects the stage at which abstract orthographic representations — specifically, letters and letter clusters — are mapped onto higher-level, location-invariant form representations, prior to full lexical access. Critically, N250 amplitude was found to vary as a graded function of the degree of orthographic overlap between prime and target: it was most attenuated for full identity repetitions, intermediate for primes sharing all but one letter with the target, and largest for fully unrelated pairs (Holcomb & Grainger, 2006; 2007). This sensitivity to partial orthographic overlap is precisely what one would expect of a component operating at the sub-lexical level, where individual letter identities and their positions are being processed, rather than whole-word representations.

Further support for a sub-lexical locus comes from the stimulus specificity of the N250. The component is robustly elicited by words and pseudowords, but not by pictures of objects, individual letters, or auditory targets (Eddy et al., 2006; Petit et al., 2006; Kiyonaga et al., 2007). This pattern of selectivity suggests that the N250 does not simply reflect low-level visual processing, nor does it reflect fully lexical or semantic processing; rather, it is specific to the processing of multi-letter strings — exactly the domain of sub-lexical orthographic computation.

The N250 has also been argued to capture sub-lexical phonological processing, in addition to orthographic processing. Grainger, Kiyonaga, and Holcomb (2006) demonstrated that the N250 may consist of separable early and late subcomponents: the earlier portion appears to be driven by orthographic overlap, while the later portion is sensitive to phonological overlap, as evidenced by differential responses to pseudohomophone primes. This finding aligns with dual-route architectures of visual word recognition, in which sub-lexical orthographic processing and sub-lexical phonological processing proceed in parallel before converging on whole-word representations (Grainger & Holcomb, 2009).

The sub-lexical interpretation of the N250 is further reinforced by its dissociation from the N400. While the N250 is modulated by both words and pseudowords in masked priming, N400 priming effects are typically restricted to real words, reflecting lexical and semantic level processing (Kiyonaga et al., 2007). The fact that these two components respond differently as a function of lexical status provides strong evidence that they index qualitatively distinct levels of processing, with the N250 reflecting an earlier, sub-lexical stage (Holcomb & Grainger, 2006; Grainger & Holcomb, 2009).

This interpretation has proven robust across different populations and writing systems. Studies of developing readers have shown that N250 priming effects are correlated with reading ability, implicating sub-lexical orthographic representations in the acquisition of skilled reading (Eddy et al., 2016). Cross-script studies in Japanese have similarly observed N250 modulation during priming across syllabary scripts, suggesting that the sub-lexical processes indexed by this component are not specific to alphabetic orthographies (Kiyonaga et al., 2013). Taken together, these findings converge on the conclusion that the N250 is a reliable electrophysiological marker of sub-lexical processing, reflecting the mapping of letter-level orthographic and phonological codes onto higher-order form representations, prior to full lexical access.

## The N400 as an Index of Semantic Processing

The N400 is one of the most extensively studied components in cognitive neuroscience, and its use as a marker of semantic processing is well established across more than four decades of ERP research. First described by Kutas and Hillyard (1980), the N400 is a negative-going component peaking around 400 ms after stimulus onset, with a broad scalp distribution most clearly visible over centro-parietal electrodes. Since its discovery, N400 amplitude has been shown to be sensitive to a wide variety of stimulus and context manipulations, including semantic priming, cloze probability, lexical association, concreteness, and semantic richness, while remaining relatively insensitive to manipulations that preserve meaning, such as physical changes in font or syntactic violations. This profile of sensitivity and insensitivity makes the N400 a particularly diagnostic measure of semantic-level processing.

The discovery of the N400 arose from investigations into how sentence context shapes word recognition. Kutas and Hillyard (1980) found that sentence-final words that were semantically anomalous (e.g., *I take my coffee with cream and dog*) elicited a large negativity peaking around 400 ms, with a broad parietally maximal scalp distribution — a response that was largest for semantic anomalies but also present for improbable yet sensible endings. Subsequent work extended this finding beyond sentence contexts, demonstrating that the N400 is robustly elicited by isolated words and word pairs. Subsequent experiments demonstrated an N400 to every word in a sentence, as well as in response to letter strings in word lists or word pairs. Critically for the present study, the N400 effect has also been reliably elicited by lexical decision tasks in which a single word prime provides a semantic context for a target word, with larger N400 amplitudes for semantically unrelated targets than for related ones at centroparietal scalp sites.

The sensitivity of the N400 to single-word semantic relationships makes it well suited as a dependent measure in studies of lexical semantic access. Words preceded by a semantically unrelated word elicit a larger N400 than those preceded by related words, and this effect is thought to reflect early automatic semantic activation. This graded sensitivity to semantic relatedness, rather than an all-or-nothing response, positions the N400 as a continuous index of the ease or difficulty of retrieving and integrating a word's meaning. The N400's broad sensitivity to meaningful stimuli and semantic manipulations has made it a primary tool for investigating how meaning-related information is stored in semantic memory, including studies of typicality, concreteness, and word class differences.

Importantly, the N400 is dissociable from components indexing earlier stages of lexical processing. As discussed above, the N250 reflects sub-lexical mapping processes that precede full lexical access (Holcomb & Grainger, 2006), while the N400 is sensitive to processing at the interface between whole-word form representations and semantic content (Grainger & Holcomb, 2009). This dissociation is supported by masked priming studies showing that N250 priming effects arise for both words and pseudowords, whereas N400 priming effects are selective to real words with semantic representations (Kiyonaga et al., 2007). The two components thus reflect qualitatively distinct processing stages, making them complementary dependent measures for studying the cascade from orthographic decoding to meaning access.

The N400 has now been elicited across an impressive range of stimulus types — including written, spoken, and signed words; drawings, photos, and videos of objects and actions — highlighting its role as a general index of meaning processing that extends beyond any single modality or task. In the context of the present study, however, its use is motivated specifically by its well-established sensitivity to the semantic properties of individual written words, making it an appropriate real-time measure of lexical semantic access during single-word processing.
