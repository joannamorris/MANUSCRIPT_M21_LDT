# Integrated Interpretation of RT and ERP Findings

We examined how morphological structure and lexical familiarity shape word recognition and how these effects vary with individual differences in reading skill. Behavioral (RT) measures establish what is easier or harder to process; ERPs reveal how those influences unfold over time. Together, the results converge on a picture in which morphological information contributes at multiple processing stages, with distinct roles for base frequency and family size, and with clear modulation by reader skill—especially by Language Proficiency.

## Reaction Time (RT) findings

### Words

RTs to words showed robust facilitation from both **Base Frequency** and **Family Size**. Participants responded faster to words derived from high-frequency bases and to words from large morphological families, with each factor contributing independently; the absence of a Base Frequency × Family Size interaction indicates additive influences. The size of these effects was practically meaningful (on the order of ~20 ms per factor), consistent with the idea that base-level familiarity supports rapid lexical access while family-level connectivity supplies additional activation that speeds recognition. As a between-subjects factor, **Orthographic Sensitivity** showed a tendency toward generally faster responding among high-sensitivity readers, whereas **Language Proficiency** did not produce a reliable main effect on word RTs. Overall, lexical decisions for real words appear primarily governed by item-level properties (base frequency and family size), with only modest contributions from individual differences to overall speed.

### Nonwords

For nonwords, **Morphological Complexity** produced a strong cost: morphologically structured items (formed from real morphemes) were rejected more slowly than matched unstructured controls, indicating an obligatory pull from morphological parsing even in the absence of a lexical entry. In contrast to the word results, **Base Frequency** was associated with *slower* rejections when the embedded base was more frequent—consistent with the idea that stronger base activation can mislead the system toward a word-like interpretation and delay “no” responses. **Family Size** did not reliably affect nonword RTs. At the individual-difference level, **Orthographic Sensitivity** conferred a general speed advantage (faster responses overall), whereas **Language Proficiency** yielded only limited modulation of nonword RTs. Thus, when lexical status is absent, morphological structure can impede decision making, and individual differences play a secondary role behaviorally.

## ERP findings

### N250 (early orthographic–morphological parsing)

The N250 results point to early sensitivity to morphological structure that is **selectively tuned by Language Proficiency**. For words, a higher-order interaction among **Family Size**, **Base Frequency**, and **Language Proficiency** indicated that only high-proficiency readers showed differential N250 responses to base frequency contingent on family size. This pattern suggests that proficient readers exploit morpho-orthographic structure efficiently, combining base-level and network-level cues early in processing. For nonwords, **low-proficiency** readers exhibited **larger complexity effects**—especially when the nonwords were built from bases in large families—consistent with broader, less selective decomposition efforts when form cues are strong but lexical support is absent. In short, proficiency appears to sharpen early morpho-orthographic parsing for words and reduce unnecessary decomposition for items that lack legitimate lexical entries.

### N400 (semantic integration and lexical–semantic activation)

The N400 findings highlight a later stage in which semantic activation and morphological connectivity interact—and again, **Language Proficiency** modulates these dynamics. For words, **Family Size** and **Base Frequency** interacted, with the pattern further moderated by proficiency: high-proficiency readers showed larger—and in places directionally distinct—frequency effects as a function of family size, indicating that they integrate base-level familiarity with family-level semantic richness more effectively. For nonwords, a **Complexity × Family Size × Language Proficiency** interaction emerged. High-proficiency readers effectively **neutralized** the typical complexity penalty when the embedded bases belonged to large families, suggesting that rich morphological networks can be leveraged to constrain interpretation even in the absence of a lexical entry. By contrast, low-proficiency readers showed **exaggerated** N400 complexity effects for large-family nonwords, consistent with reliance on familiar morphemic cues without successfully suppressing them when they are misleading.

## Cross-measure synthesis

Three cross-cutting conclusions follow from the RT and ERP convergence:

1. **Dual roles for morphological information.**
   Base frequency and family size facilitate recognition for real words (RT facilitation; N250/N400 sensitivity), yet the same cues can hinder nonword rejection (RT slowing; enlarged N250/N400 responses for structured imposters). Morphological parsing appears obligatory enough to intrude on “no” decisions, especially when base activation is strong.

2. **Stage-specific modulation by proficiency.**
   Language Proficiency exerts little direct influence on word RTs but **strongly shapes ERP indices**: proficient readers show more selective early parsing (N250) and more flexible semantic integration (N400), aligning with accounts in which skill optimizes how orthographic and morphological information is weighted over time.

3. **Orthographic Sensitivity as a general speed factor.**
   Orthographic Sensitivity primarily shifts overall response speed (faster RTs) with limited interaction with item-level morphology in the ERP data, suggesting that it reflects efficiency in lower-level encoding and decision processes rather than in the integration of morphological and semantic information.

## Theoretical implications

These findings support **interactive models** of visual word recognition in which morphological signals are available early and feed forward into semantic activation while remaining subject to top-down constraint from lexical knowledge and reader skill. For words, **Base Frequency** likely accelerates initial access to the morphemic stem, while **Family Size** provides network-level support (co-activation of related derivations) that eases integration—effects that are visible both in faster RTs and in N250/N400 modulations. For nonwords, the same machinery can temporarily misguide the system: high-frequency bases and large families promote “word-like” activation, slowing rejections (RT) and increasing processing demands (ERPs). **Language Proficiency** determines how efficiently this activation is marshaled: proficient readers appear better at exploiting morphological structure when it is helpful and suppressing it when it misleads, yielding selective N250 effects and flexible N400 integration. **Orthographic Sensitivity**, in contrast, behaves more like a global processing-speed factor with limited impact on the architecture of morphological integration.

## Practical significance and boundary conditions

Methodologically, the additive Base Frequency and Family Size effects for words argue against over-reducing morphology to a single familiarity dimension; both stem-level and network-level variables matter. The absence of a Family Size effect on nonword RTs underscores that network benefits depend on lexical legitimacy, even though ERPs reveal underlying engagement with morphological cues. Finally, the proficiency-linked N250/N400 interactions caution against averaging across heterogeneous readers: early morpho-orthographic parsing and later semantic integration are not uniform processes but are calibrated by skill.

---

**In sum**, morphological information shapes recognition at multiple levels. Words benefit from both base familiarity and family connectivity; nonwords suffer when those same cues pull the system toward spurious lexical activation. Proficient readers manage these cues more selectively across time—visible as tuned N250 parsing and adaptive N400 integration—while orthographic sensitivity primarily speeds responses without substantially reconfiguring how morphology is integrated.
