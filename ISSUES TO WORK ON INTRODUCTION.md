This is a well-structured, clearly written introduction. The logical arc is sound and the prose is genuinely good. But here is what a picky reviewer would flag:

---

**Section 1 (Morphological Structure and Lexical Familiarity)**

This is the weakest section relative to the paper's actual contribution, for the reasons we already discussed. Specifically:

- You cite Taft (1979) and Schreuder (1997) but no subsequent decades of work. A reviewer will notice that the morphological processing literature has moved considerably since 1997 — Rastle, Davis, Marslen-Wilson, Baayen and colleagues have produced highly relevant work on decomposition. The citation base here will read as thin.
- You introduce "automatic morphological parsing" as if it is settled, but this is theoretically contested. You are implicitly committed to a full-decomposition view without acknowledging alternatives. This matters because your ERP predictions depend on a particular architecture.
- The nonword paragraph is fine but undercited. "Reliably processed differently" needs a reference or two.
- Crucially, **individual differences are completely absent from this section**, despite being the paper's central contribution. A reviewer will notice the disconnect between this section and the rest of the intro immediately.

---

**Section 2 (Behavioral Versus Neural Sensitivity)**

This section is well-motivated and the logic is clean. Two issues:

- The N250 and N400 descriptions are asserted rather than grounded. You should cite the key empirical work that established these components' roles in morphological processing — at minimum Appelhauser/Grainger for N250 and Kutas & Federmeier for N400. Without citations here, a reviewer may ask whether these characterizations are accurate or selective.
- The final sentence ("distinguish effects that reflect general processing efficiency from those that reveal differences in how and when morphological structure influences word recognition") is slightly circular — it restates the logic rather than advancing it. This paragraph could end more crisply by pointing forward to individual differences specifically.

---

**Section 3 (Individual Differences)**

This is the strongest section. The Orthographic Sensitivity / Language Proficiency distinction is clearly drawn and the stage-specificity logic is compelling. However:

- The Cronbach citation is doing a lot of work alone. The claim that individual differences are treated as noise is worth a sentence of elaboration — are there more recent citations supporting the move *toward* systematic individual differences approaches in reading research? Reviewers may want to see that this is a current debate, not just a historical straw man.
- The section makes confident theoretical predictions without citing the empirical literature that motivates them. What prior work shows that orthographic sensitivity predicts early processing, or that vocabulary predicts N400 effects? Even one or two citations per claim would substantially strengthen this section.
- You describe how these dimensions were derived (PCA of vocabulary, spelling, print exposure) in the abstract but not here in the intro. A reader encountering the intro fresh will not know what Language Proficiency and Orthographic Sensitivity are operationally until the Methods section. A brief forward pointer or parenthetical here would help.

---

**Section 4 (Words Versus Nonwords)**

Solid and well-reasoned. One concern:

- This section does a good job motivating nonwords theoretically, but it floats somewhat free of prior empirical work. Has anyone actually shown that individual differences are *larger* for nonwords than words? If yes, cite it — it would powerfully support the design logic. If not, you should acknowledge that this is a prediction rather than an established finding, which is fine, but should be framed accordingly.

---

**Section 5 (The Present Study)**

Clear and well-organized. Two things:

- The predictions are stated confidently ("we predicted that Orthographic Sensitivity would primarily influence early processing efficiency... reflected in faster response times and modulation of the N250"). Given that your abstract reports *little evidence that individual differences selectively modulated morphological effects* in behavior, the RT prediction is not confirmed. A reviewer will notice the mismatch. You might soften this slightly or add a hedge — "we predicted... though we anticipated this might be more apparent in neural than behavioral measures."
- The final paragraph describing the organization of results is useful but unusually long and mechanical for an introduction. Most journals do not require this kind of roadmap, and it slightly dilutes the ending. Consider trimming it to two sentences.

---

**Overall**

The introduction would be considerably strengthened by: (1) integrating individual differences into the first section; (2) adding citations to anchor the N250/N400 characterizations and the theoretical predictions in section 3; (3) softening the RT prediction to match your actual findings; and (4) trimming the final roadmap paragraph. The prose itself needs little work — the issues are structural and citational rather than stylistic.

Here is a passage you could insert at the end of the nonword paragraph, or as a brief stand-alone bridge before moving to ERPs:

---

Whether these effects reflect the retrieval of explicit morpheme representations or emergent sensitivity to statistical regularities among word forms remains theoretically contested \citep{stevensPlaut2022}. On decompositional accounts, readers segment strings into discrete morphological units that are accessed and combined during recognition. On connectionist accounts, no such segmentation step is required; morphological regularities are instead captured implicitly through learned covariance among orthographic and semantic patterns, with morpheme-like behavior emerging as a consequence of that structure rather than its cause. Current behavioral and electrophysiological methods are unlikely to fully adjudicate between these positions, since both predict sensitivity to morphological structure in processing. The present study therefore remains agnostic on this question. What the design does allow us to ask is whether *whatever process underlies early sensitivity to morphological structure* varies systematically as a function of reader skill — a question that is meaningful under either theoretical framework.
