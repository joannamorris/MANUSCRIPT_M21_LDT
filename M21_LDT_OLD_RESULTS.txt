\subsection{Behavioral Results}

A linear mixed-effects model was fit to predict response time as a function of standardized log base-10 bigram frequency (\textit{Log10BF\_std}), standardized morphological family size (\textit{FS\_std}), and their interaction. The model included a random intercept for participant (\textit{SubjID}) and was estimated using restricted maximum likelihood with Kenward-Roger approximated degrees of freedom.

Significant main effects were observed for both \textit{Log10BF\_std}, $F$(1, 6234.71) = 49.68, $p$ < .001, and \textit{FS\_std}, $F$(1, 6234.47) = 21.80, $p$ < .001. The interaction between \textit{Log10BF\_std} and \textit{FS\_std} was not significant, $F$(1, 6234.45) = 2.53, $p$ = .112. Effect sizes for the main effects were small: partial $\eta^2 = .0079$ for \textit{Log10BF\_std} (95\% CI [0.00, 1.00]) and partial $\eta^2 = .0035$ for \textit{FS\_std} (95\% CI [0.00, 1.00]). The interaction had a negligible effect size (partial $\eta^2 = .0004$, 95\% CI [0.00, 1.00]).

Fixed-effect estimates indicated that response time decreased as bigram frequency increased, $b$ = --10.53, $SE$ = 1.49, $t$(6234.53) = --7.05, $p$ < .001, and as morphological family size increased, $b$ = --6.76, $SE$ = 1.45, $t$(6234.29) = --4.67, $p$ < .001. The interaction term was not statistically significant, $b$ = 2.68, $SE$ = 1.68, $t$(6234.26) = 1.59, $p$ = .112.

The model explained 30.8\% of the total variance in response time ($R^2_\text{conditional} = .308$), with fixed effects alone accounting for 1.1\% of the variance ($R^2_\text{marginal} = .011$). Random effects for participants accounted for a substantial proportion of variability (intercept variance = 5310, SD = 72.87; residual variance = 12351, SD = 111.13). The analysis was based on 6{,}308 observations across 71 participants.

\paragraph{Word Data.} To examine the effects of base frequency, morphological family size, and orthographic sensitivity on lexical decision response times to words, we fit a linear mixed-effects model with fixed effects of log-transformed base frequency (\textit{Log}$_{10}$BF), log-transformed family size (\textit{Log}$_{10}$FS), orthographic sensitivity (High vs. Low), and their interactions, along with a random intercept for subject. The model revealed significant main effects of base frequency, $F(1, 5792.77) = 46.75$, $p < .001$, and family size, $F(1, 5792.45) = 29.26$, $p < .001$. The main effect of orthographic sensitivity approached significance, $F(1, 64.04) = 3.12$, $p = .082$.

None of the two-way or three-way interactions reached significance (all $p > .13$), although follow-up analyses of estimated simple slopes showed that the effect of base frequency was numerically larger for participants with high orthographic sensitivity ($b = -12.86$, 95\% CI [$-17.5$, $-8.21$]) compared to those with low sensitivity ($b = -8.24$, 95\% CI [$-12.1$, $-4.37$]); however, the difference between these slopes was not significant, $t(5793) = -1.50$, $p = .135$.

Estimated marginal means at the mean of both continuous predictors indicated that participants with high orthographic sensitivity responded faster overall ($M = 590.91$ ms, $SE = 13.94$) than those with low sensitivity ($M = 623.35$ ms, $SE = 11.97$), though this difference was not statistically significant.

\paragraph{Nonword Data.} 

A chi-square test of independence revealed a significant association between nonword complexity and morphological family size, $\chi^2(1, N = 4671) = 3.93$, $p = .047$. Nonwords with complex morphology were slightly more likely to be based on stems from small morphological families compared to simple nonwords.

Reaction times to nonwords were analyzed using a linear mixed-effects model with raw RTs (in milliseconds) as the dependent variable. Fixed effects included standardized log base frequency (LogBF), standardized log morphological family size (Log$_{10}$FS), and orthographic sensitivity group, along with all two- and three-way interactions. A random intercept was included for each participant. This model examined whether frequency-based lexical variables influenced the processing of morphologically structured nonwords in a similar manner to real words.

Significant main effects were observed for both base frequency, $F(1, 4600.23) = 28.51$, $p < .001$, and morphological family size, $F(1, 4599.75) = 4.69$, $p = .030$, indicating that participants responded more quickly to nonwords derived from more frequent stems and larger morphological families. The main effect of orthographic sensitivity was not significant, $F(1, 64.89) = 2.69$, $p = .106$, nor were any interaction terms.

A trend-level base frequency $\times$ family size interaction was observed, $F(1, 4599.64) = 2.96$, $p = .085$. To assess the contribution of interaction terms, we compared the full model (with all two- and three-way interactions) to a reduced model omitting interactions involving orthographic sensitivity. A likelihood ratio test showed that the interaction terms did not significantly improve model fit, $\chi^2(3) = 0.81$, $p = .848$.

To further characterize the observed effects, we estimated marginal trends across participants using \texttt{emtrends()}. The base frequency effect was significant, $b = 10.45$, $SE = 1.96$, 95\% CI [6.61, 14.30], as was the effect of morphological family size, $b = -4.30$, $SE = 1.98$, 95\% CI [--8.19, --0.41], indicating faster responses to nonwords with higher-frequency stems and larger morphological families. These effects did not differ by group. The base frequency effect was slightly stronger in the Low Orthographic group ($b = 11.07$) than in the High Orthographic group ($b = 9.82$), but the difference was not significant, $t(4600) = 0.32$, $p = .749$.

Estimated marginal means of RT at the mean of both predictors were 2760.6~ms [95\% CI: 2738, 2783] for the High Orthographic group and 2784.8~ms [95\% CI: 2766, 2804] for the Low Orthographic group. This small numerical difference was not statistically reliable.

\paragraph{Behavioral Data Summary}. Participants responded more quickly to words and nonwords that contained more frequent stems and belonged to larger morphological families, suggesting that both base frequency and family structure facilitate processing. While orthographic sensitivity was associated with overall faster responses to words, it did not significantly modulate the effects of frequency or family size in either condition.



\subsection{N250 Results for Nonword Data}

A chi-square test of independence revealed a significant association between nonword complexity and morphological family size, $\chi^2(1, N = 4671) = 3.93$, $p = .047$. Nonwords with complex morphology were slightly more likely to be based on stems from small morphological families compared to simple nonwords.

A linear mixed-effects modeling approach was used to analyze ERP amplitudes in the N250 time window. This method offers several advantages over traditional repeated-measures ANOVA. First, it allows for accurate estimation of fixed effects by explicitly modeling nuisance variables such as Laterality and Anteriority, which account for a substantial proportion of the variance in ERP amplitude. Including these topographic covariates in the model improves the precision of estimates for the linguistic variables of interest. Second, mixed-effects models provide a more appropriate treatment of within-subject dependencies by estimating random effects for participants, thereby avoiding the sphericity assumptions required by repeated-measures ANOVA and allowing for unbalanced data without biasing results. Third, the use of planned contrasts and post hoc comparisons with Bonferroni correction helps control the family-wise error rate, reducing the risk of Type I error across multiple tests. Finally, by treating subjects as random effects, the model supports broader generalization of the findings beyond the specific sample tested. Together, these features make linear mixed-effects models well suited for analyzing ERP data with complex factorial designs and repeated observations.

A linear mixed-effects model was fitted to ERP amplitudes in the N250 time window, with fixed effects of sensitivity to orthographic structure, morphological family size, morphological complexity, and their interactions. Laterality and anteriority were included as nuisance covariates to control for scalp distribution effects, and random intercepts were estimated for participants. Degrees of freedom and p-values were computed using the Kenward-Roger approximation. The model explained 42.1\% of the total variance ($R^2_\text{conditional} = .421$), with fixed effects accounting for 2.5\% of the variance ($R^2_\text{marginal} = .025$).

No significant main effects were found for sensitivity to orthographic structure, family size, or complexity $p$ values > $.48$), nor for the nuisance variables laterality and anteriority. However, two significant two-way interactions emerged.

First, the interaction between sensitivity to orthographic structure and morphological family size was significant, $F(1, 2121) = 4.52$, $p = .034$, $\eta^2_{\text{partial}} = .0021$. Although no individual pairwise comparisons survived Bonferroni correction, the pattern of estimated marginal means revealed a crossover trend: For participants with low sensitivity to orthographic structure, items from large families elicited more negative amplitudes ($M = -0.74~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.33, 95\% CI [–1.41, –0.08]) than items from small families ($M = -0.61~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.33, 95\% CI [–1.27, 0.06]), $t(2121) = -1.05$, $p = 1.000$, $d = -0.049$, 95\% CI [–0.16, 0.07]. For participants with high sensitivity to orthographic structure, the direction reversed, with large family items evoking less negative amplitudes ($M = -0.37~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.36, 95\% CI [–1.09, 0.35]) than small family items ($M = -0.64~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.36, 95\% CI [–1.36, 0.08]), $t(2121) = 1.92$, $p = .327$, $d = 0.089$, 95\% CI [–0.03, 0.21].

Second, the interaction between sensitivity to orthographic structure and morphological complexity was also significant, $F(1, 2121) = 6.14$, $p = .013$, $\eta^2_{\text{partial}} = .0029$. Similarly, no individual pairwise comparisons survived Bonferroni correction $p$ values > $.08$), but the pattern of means revealed a crossover interaction. For participants with high sensitivity to orthographic structure, complex items evoked more negative amplitudes ($M = -0.61~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.36, 95\% CI [–1.33, 0.11]) than simple items ($M = -0.40~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.36, 95\% CI [–1.12, 0.32]), $t(2121) = -1.49$, $p = .273$, $d = -0.069$, 95\% CI [–0.19, 0.05]. For participants with low sensitivity to orthographic structure, the direction reversed: complex items were somewhat less negative ($M = -0.54~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.33, 95\% CI [–1.21, 0.12]) than simple items ($M = -0.81~\mu\text{V}$, SE = 0.33, 95\% CI [–1.47, –0.14]), $t(2121) = 2.04$, $p = .083$, $d = 0.094$, 95\% CI [–0.02, 0.21].

The three-way interaction among sensitivity to orthographic structure, morphological family size, and complexity was not significant, $F(1, 2121) = 0.14$, $p = .708$, $\eta^2_{\text{partial}} < .0001$.

To further evaluate the contribution of interaction terms, nested model comparisons were conducted by removing each interaction and any higher-order terms that included it. These comparisons did not yield a significant improvement in model fit (all ps = 1.0), consistent with the small effect sizes and known limitations of likelihood ratio tests in detecting subtle effects in complex or unbalanced designs. Therefore, we rely on the Kenward-Roger corrected F-tests as the primary basis for inference regarding fixed effects.

\subsection{N400 Results (300--500 ms)}


ERP amplitudes in the N400 time window were analyzed using the same linear mixed-effects model structure applied to other time windows. The model included fixed effects of sensitivity to orthographic structure, morphological family size, morphological complexity, and their interactions. Laterality and anteriority were included as nuisance covariates to control for scalp distribution effects, and random intercepts were estimated for participants. Degrees of freedom and p-values were computed using the Kenward-Roger approximation. The model explained 51.0\% of the total variance ($R^2_\text{conditional} = .510$), with fixed effects accounting for 7.6\% of the variance ($R^2_\text{marginal} = .076$).

No significant main effects or interactions involving orthographic sensitivity, morphological family size, or morphological complexity were observed ($p$s > .19), and all associated partial effect sizes were very small ($\eta^2_{\text{partial}} < .004$). The only significant effects in the model were related to scalp topography: ERP amplitudes varied by laterality, $F(2, 2121) = 4.66$, $p = .010$, and anteriority, $F(2, 2121) = 135.07$, $p < .001$, consistent with the typical distribution of the N400 component. These effects reflect spatial variability in the signal but do not indicate modulations by the experimental manipulations. In sum, we found no effects of morphological complexity, family size, or orthographic sensitivity on N400 amplitude.
